SYNOPSIS
Executive Summary
No single graph schema survives contact with a cross-sector SEC corpus. A schema designed around
Apple’s clean multi-step income statement will reject JPMorgan’s unclassified balance sheet; a
schema tuned for ExxonMobil’s production-cost taxonomy will mis-handle Pfizer’s
COGS-plus-R&D-and-settlements; structure. This document presents a Universal Financial Graph
Schema (UFGS) engineered to absorb these sector-specific structural disparities without flattening
them, by maintaining a dual-track design in which every reported fact retains its raw as_filed_label
alongside a normalized canonical anchor.
The schema is the product of a four-phase analysis. Phase 1 maps the structural invariants of Forms 10-K,
10-Q, and 8-K—their item hierarchies, section boundary markers, and footnote linkage patterns—across four
GICS sectors (Technology, Banking, Healthcare, Energy). Phase 2 reconciles the accounting concept zoo: the
same economic phenomenon labeled “Total net sales” (AAPL), “Total revenue” (NVDA), “Total net revenue”
(JPM, which adds interest and non-interest income), “Total revenues” (PFE), and “Total revenues and other
income” (XOM) is normalized to a canonical StandardizedConcept:TotalRevenue while preserving the
sector-specific raw label as a first-class attribute. Forty such canonical anchors span the income statement,
balance sheet, cash flow statement, and bank-specific regulatory metrics.
Phase 3 introduces a qualitative causal layer over the narrative disclosures in Item 1A (Risk Factors) and Item
7 (MD&A;). Risk disclosures are parsed into typed relations—DRIVES, IMPACTS_MARGIN, MITIGATES,
CREATES_EXPOSURE_TO, COMPOUNDS, OFFSETS—linking macro variables (Fed Funds Rate, Export Controls,
Commodity Prices), entity types (ProductFamily, GeographicMarket, Competitor, Supplier, Customer,
RegulatoryBody), and financial outcomes (margin compression, capital allocation, credit loss provisioning).
Phase 4 codifies an Edge Case Register covering restatements (Form 10-K/A and 10-Q/A under ASC 250),
quarterly revisions (material vs. immaterial thresholds), non-calendar fiscal years (NVDA’s late-January FYE,
AAPL’s late-September FYE, XOM/PFE/JPM on December 31), and discontinued operations under ASC
205-20. Each edge case is given an explicit graph modeling recipe with worked examples drawn from the
2018–2026 corpus.
On day one, a graph architect using this schema can ingest XBRL-tagged facts from EDGAR, normalize them
through ~25 deterministic mapping rules, run cross-sector integrity constraints (e.g., TotalAssets =
TotalLiabilities + TotalEquity for non-banks; LoansHeldForInvestment + AllowanceForCreditLosses =
NetLoans for banks), and execute cross-sector queries such as “which companies in the sample saw R&D; as a
percentage of revenue grow fastest over the 2020–2024 window, normalized for fiscal year alignment.” The
schema is deliberately not exhaustive—four sectors and three form types—but the patterns it codifies extend
cleanly to IFRS filers, foreign private issuer 20-Fs, and additional sector overlays (REIT, Insurance,
Broker-Dealer) without restructuring.
Reading guide. Chapter 1 establishes scope and corpus. Chapter 2 details Phase 1 structural invariants.
Chapter 3 covers Phase 2 accounting reconciliation and presents the forty-concept anchor list. Chapter 4
covers Phase 3 causal taxonomy. Chapters 5–7 are the three formal deliverables: the Sector Invariant Matrix,

CHAPTER ONE
1. Introduction & Scope
1.1 The Cross-Sector Schema Problem
The Securities and Exchange Commission’s EDGAR system contains more than twenty million filings, the
bulk of which are structured financial reports—Forms 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K—filed by U.S. domestic
registrants under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. Since 2019, these filings have been required to carry
inline XBRL markup, which in principle makes every reported financial fact machine-readable. In practice,
the XBRL US GAAP taxonomy contains over 18,000 tags and a single economic concept—say,
“revenue”—may be represented by any of a dozen tags depending on the filer’s accounting policy, sector, and
reporting tradition. A graph architect attempting to build a unified analytical layer over EDGAR faces a
paradox: the data is structured, but the structure is not unified.
The problem compounds across sectors. A commercial bank’s balance sheet is unclassified—there is no
“current vs. non-current” split because the bank’s assets and liabilities are predominantly financial instruments
whose liquidity is managed on a different temporal logic. A technology company’s balance sheet, by contrast,
follows the classic ASC 210 classified structure with explicit current/non-current segregation. An energy
major’s income statement leads with “Total revenues and other income”—a construction that aggregates
upstream sales, downstream refining margins, and corporate-level items that a pharma company would never
commingle. Each sector’s filings are internally coherent; cross-sector joins break.
This document constructs a schema that absorbs these disparities by design. The core design principle is
dual-track preservation: every financial fact carries both its raw as_filed_label (the XBRL tag and
human-readable label exactly as the filer reported it) and a pointer to a StandardizedConcept drawn from a
fixed canonical list of approximately forty anchors. The canonical anchors are sector-aware: where no
meaningful cross-sector mapping exists (Basel III Tier 1 Capital, for example, has no Technology or Energy
equivalent), the concept exists in the schema as a sector-specific overlay rather than being forced into a false
equivalence.

1.3 Window Rationale (2018–2026)
The sample window is chosen to span five inflection points that stress any graph schema. First, the 2020
adoption of Current Expected Credit Losses (CECL, ASU 2016-13) restructured how banks provision for
credit losses, replacing the incurred-loss model with a forward-looking expected-loss model; any bank-sector
schema must represent both regimes and the transition. Second, the COVID-19 disclosures of 2020–2021
introduced extraordinary MD&A; and Risk Factor narrative content (supply chain disruption, government
stimulus, remote-work operational impact) that no schema built on pre-2020 data would anticipate. Third, the
Federal Reserve’s 2022–2023 rate-hike cycle compressed net interest margins across the banking sector while
expanding interest income for cash-rich technology issuers—a divergence the schema must reconcile under
one canonical concept set.
Fourth, the 2024–2026 surge in AI-related capital expenditure, most acutely at NVIDIA (FY ending late
January, so the FY24 10-K filed February 2024 already shows the inflection) and at hyperscaler customers
(Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet, Amazon), introduces sector-specific capex narratives that touch supply
commitment disclosures (Item 1 supply commitments in AAPL’s case, multi-billion-dollar purchase
obligations in NVDA’s case). Fifth, the SEC’s 2023 modernization of Item 1A risk factor disclosure
rules—permitting more concise, prioritized risk discussion—changes the structural profile of risk narratives
mid-corpus. Any schema that does not accommodate both pre- and post-2023 risk-factor formatting will fail
on roughly half the sample.
1.4 What “Universal” Means

------------------------------------------------------------

Universal is not a synonym for one-size-fits-all. A schema that forces every sector into a single canonical
structure will either drop information (losing the unclassified bank balance sheet’s temporal liquidity structure)
or fabricate information (inventing a “current asset” classification that the filer never reported). The UFGS
design is layered: a universal base layer captures the concepts that genuinely recur across all sectors (revenue,
net income, total assets, total equity, operating cash flow); a sector overlay layer carries the concepts that exist
only within a particular sector (Basel III capital ratios for banks, segment-level production volumes for energy,
R&D; pipeline milestones for pharma); a raw layer preserves every reported fact with its full XBRL lineage.
The architecture is summarized in Chapter 8 and operationalized in Chapter 9.
CHAPTER TWO
2. Phase 1 — Structural & Section Taxonomy
Invariants
Before any financial fact can be normalized, the schema must know where in a filing it lives. SEC
forms are not flat documents; they are deeply nested hierarchies with regulated item codes, and the
same economic fact may appear in different item locations depending on the form type and reporting
period. This chapter maps the structural invariants across Forms 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K—the
document hierarchies, the section boundary markers, and the footnote linkage patterns that connect
reported line items to their underlying detail.
2.1 Form 10-K Anatomy
The annual report on Form 10-K is the most structurally complex of the three form types. Under Regulation
S-K and Regulation S-X, a 10-K is organized into sixteen parts and items, though in practice the back-half
items (Items 10–16) are incorporated by reference from the proxy statement and appear only as a pointer in
the 10-K body. The schema treats these as a single section type with a forwarding flag rather than as absent
content. The front-half items carry the substantive disclosure: business overview, risk factors, property, legal
proceedings, market for registrant’s common equity, selected financial data (historically; this item was largely
eliminated by the 2021 FAST Act amendments for smaller reporting companies), MD&A;, quantitative and
qualitative market risk disclosures, financial statements, changes in disagreements with accountants, controls
and procedures.

2.2 Form 10-Q Anatomy
The quarterly report on Form 10-Q is structurally simpler than the 10-K and follows a two-part architecture
mandated by Reg S-K. Part I covers Financial Information in four items: the condensed consolidated financial
statements (Item 1), management’s discussion and analysis (Item 2), quantitative and qualitative market risk
disclosures (Item 3), and controls and procedures (Item 4). Part II covers Other Information in six items: legal

------------------------------------------------------------

proceedings (Item 1), unregistered sales of equity securities (Item 2), material defaults on senior securities
(Item 3), mine safety disclosures (Item 4, energy sector only), other information (Item 5), and exhibits (Item
6). The 10-Q’s MD&A; in Item 2 is structurally parallel to the 10-K’s Item 7 but typically covers a three-month
and year-to-date comparison rather than a full-year retrospective.
A critical schema invariant: 10-Q financial statements are explicitly unaudited. Every RawFact extracted from
a 10-Q carries an audit_status=unaudited attribute, distinguishing it from 10-K facts which carry
audit_status=pcAOB_audited. This distinction is essential for restatement handling (see Chapter 7): when a
10-Q fact is later restated in a 10-K/A, the schema must preserve the original unaudited fact with a revision
edge rather than overwriting it.
2.3 Form 8-K Event Taxonomy
Form 8-K is the SEC’s mechanism for unscheduled material event disclosure. Unlike the periodic 10-K and
10-Q, which follow a fixed item structure, the 8-K has a topic-based taxonomy spanning nine sections with
approximately thirty sub-items. The schema must parse the 8-K as an event stream rather than as a structured
report. Each 8-K filing may report multiple items simultaneously—for example, an acquisition announcement
typically files Items 2.01 (completion of acquisition), 7.01 (Reg FD disclosure), and 9.01 (financial statements
and exhibits) in a single submission.

2.4 Cross-Form Alignment
The three form types are not independent: a 10-K’s Item 7 MD&A; references quarterly 10-Q Item 2 MD&A;
disclosures; an 8-K Item 2.02 results announcement precedes the corresponding 10-Q filing; an 8-K Item 5.02
executive departure may trigger a 10-K Item 10 refresh via proxy filing. The schema models these as
cross-form edges of type SUPERSEDES, CONFIRMS, or REFINES. For example, an 8-K Item 2.02 filed on
January 25 announcing Q4 preliminary results will be linked to the subsequent 10-K (filed late February) via a
CONFIRMS edge from the 8-K’s preliminary RawFact node to the 10-K’s audited RawFact node.
2.5 Section Boundary Markers
In HTML and XBRL filings, sections are delimited by a mix of structural anchors: HTML heading tags,
page-break anchors, XBRL [context] elements, and SEC-specific document tags. The schema’s ingestion
pipeline applies a sequence of regex and structural-pattern matchers to extract section boundaries. The primary
anchors are:
• PART I / PART II all-caps headers in 10-Q and 10-K body text
• ITEM 1 through ITEM 9C numbered item headers; the schema allows both “Item 1.” and “ITEM 1.” styles
• XBRL [us-gaap:StatementLineItems] containers mark the boundaries of financial statement tables
• XBRL [context] elements with instant or duration period types define the temporal scope of every
fact
• HTML [a name="md_and_a"] or equivalent named anchors used by some filers (notably AAPL and
MSFT) to demarcate MD&A; sections
2.6 Footnote Linkage
Financial statement line items reference footnotes (e.g., “Note 1”, “Note 12”) that carry the substantive detail.
In XBRL, this linkage is captured via the [link:footnoteLink] element which connects a [loc] (locator for
the line item) to a [footnote] resource via an arcrole of
http://www.xbrl.org/2003/arcrole/fact-footnote. The schema preserves this linkage as a
DISCLOSED_IN edge from the RawFact node to a Footnote node carrying the note number, the as-filed text,
and a sector-specific note-type tag (e.g., segment_note, commitments_note, regulatory_capital_note
for banks).
A common ingestion failure mode is to drop footnote linkage and treat each line item as atomic. This loses the
segment detail that banks and energy issuers bury in Note 20-something—for example, JPMorgan’s
segment-level VaR by business line (Consumer & Community Banking, Corporate & Investment Bank,
Commercial Banking, Asset & Wealth Management) lives in a footnote table referenced by the MD&A; but
not directly from the primary financial statements. The schema treats footnote extraction as a first-class
concern, with explicit Footnote nodes and typed edges that preserve the hierarchy.

CHAPTER THREE
3. Phase 2 — Accounting Concept Reconciliation
Phase 2 is the heart of the schema. Where Phase 1 mapped structural invariants, Phase 2 maps
accounting semantics. The objective is to define a canonical list of approximately forty
StandardizedConcept anchors that normalize income statements, balance sheets, cash flow
statements, and sector-specific regulatory metrics across all four sectors—without discarding the
raw, as-filed label that carries sector-specific information. The dual-track design preserves fidelity
for sector-specific queries while enabling cross-sector aggregations.
3.1 Revenue Variants
The single most visible label disparity across the sample is in the top line of the income statement. Apple
reports “Total net sales,” a construction that emphasizes the net-of-returns character of its revenue. NVIDIA
reports simply “Total revenue,” consistent with its contract-with-customer accounting under ASC 606.
JPMorgan reports “Total net revenue,” which is the sum of net interest income (interest income less interest
expense) plus non-interest revenue (fees, service charges, trading gains). Pfizer reports “Total revenues,”
which includes product sales, alliance revenues, contract manufacturing, and other income. ExxonMobil
reports “Total revenues and other income,” which adds equity-method affiliate income and other
non-operating items to sales revenue.
These are not merely lexical variations—each construction embeds an accounting policy decision. Apple’s “net
sales” reflects ASC 606 revenue net of estimated returns; JPMorgan’s “net revenue” reflects the bank-practice
convention of netting interest expense against interest income at the top-line level (a treatment that no
non-bank issuer would apply); ExxonMobil’s inclusion of equity-method income reflects the integrated oil
business model where upstream joint ventures (often 50%-owned) are consolidated by equity method rather
than full consolidation. The schema captures these differences as attributes of the RawFact node while

3.2 Operating Expense Variants
Operating expenses exhibit the widest cross-sector structural divergence. The non-bank sample (AAPL,
NVDA, PFE, XOM) follows the standard ASC 720 classification: cost of sales (or cost of revenue), research
and development, selling/general/administrative, and sector-specific add-ons (amortization of acquired
intangibles for PFE, exploration and production expenses for XOM, supply commitment accruals for AAPL).
The bank sample (JPM, GS) follows an entirely different taxonomy: non-interest expense, which is
decomposed into compensation expense, technology and communications, occupancy and equipment,
professional and outside services, and other. There is no R&D; line on a bank income statement—technology
investment is embedded in non-interest expense.
The schema reconciles these by mapping both taxonomies to a canonical
StandardizedConcept:TotalOperatingExpense, with sector-specific decomposition tracked via typed children.
For non-banks, the children are CostOfRevenue, ResearchAndDevelopment,
SellingGeneralAndAdministrative, and AmortizationOfIntangibles. For banks, the children are
CompensationExpense, TechnologyAndCommunications, OccupancyAndEquipment,
ProfessionalServices, and OtherNonInterestExpense. A cross-sector query such as “compensation as
percentage of total operating expense” would not be meaningful across the bank/non-bank divide and the
schema’s query layer rejects it; a query for “R&D; as percentage of revenue” is meaningful across non-banks
only.
3.3 Balance Sheet Variants
The non-bank sample follows the classified balance sheet structure mandated by ASC 210: current assets
(cash, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses), non-current assets (PP&E;, intangibles, goodwill, long-term
investments), current liabilities (accounts payable, accrued expenses, short-term debt), non-current liabilities
(long-term debt, deferred tax, pension obligations), and equity. The bank sample follows an unclassified
structure: assets are presented in order of liquidity (cash and due from banks, interest-bearing deposits with
banks, federal funds sold and securities purchased under resale agreements, trading assets, loans held for
investment, loans held for sale, premises and equipment, intangible assets, other assets), and liabilities follow
the same logic (deposits, federal funds purchased and securities sold under repurchase agreements, trading
liabilities, accounts payable and other liabilities, short-term and long-term borrowings, shareholders’ equity).
This is not a stylistic choice but a reflection of the underlying business model: a bank “current asset” in the
ASC 210 sense (expected to convert to cash within twelve months) would include a substantial fraction of the

------------------------------------------------------------

loan book, which is operationally misleading. The schema models this as a sector-specific balance sheet
structure: the RawFact nodes preserve the as-filed line items with their as-filed ordering; the
StandardizedConcept mapping layers on a normalized asset class (cash, financial_asset, operating_asset,
intangible) without forcing a current/non-current classification that the filer did not report. The integrity
constraint TotalAssets = TotalLiabilities + TotalEquity holds for both structures.

3.5 Cash Flow Statement Reconciliation
Cash flow statements are the most cross-sector-consistent of the three primary statements, which simplifies the
schema. ASC 230 mandates a three-section structure (operating, investing, financing) with limited
sector-specific variation. The schema’s four StandardizedConcepts for cash flow—SC-31 through
SC-36—cover the lines most commonly queried by analysts. Bank-specific variations include the classification
of trading asset flows (operating for banks, investing for non-banks) and the treatment of securitization
activities (typically investing). These differences are captured at the RawFact level via an
operating_classification attribute rather than at the StandardizedConcept level, preserving the canonical
SC mapping while flagging the classification difference.
3.6 Regulatory Metric Overlay
Concepts SC-37 through SC-40 cover bank-specific regulatory metrics that have no cross-sector equivalent.
The schema deliberately does not force these into the universal concept list—doing so would create empty
nodes for four-fifths of the sample. Instead, they live in a sector overlay table that is loaded only for bank

------------------------------------------------------------

filings. The same pattern applies to other sector overlays: energy-specific metrics (production volumes,
realized prices by commodity, reserves replacement ratio) live in an energy overlay; pharma-specific metrics
(pipeline stage, regulatory submission milestones, exclusivity expiration calendar) live in a pharma overlay;
technology-specific metrics (segment-level gross margin, deferred revenue, RPO) live in a tech overlay.
The overlay design is critical for query performance. A cross-sector query such as “which issuer in the sample
has the highest capital return ratio (dividends + buybacks) / free cash flow” can execute against the universal
layer alone, without joining to the bank overlay. A bank-specific query such as “CET1 ratio trajectory across
the rate-hike cycle” executes against the bank overlay joined to the universal layer for company and filing
context. This separation prevents schema bloat and keeps the universal layer minimal and predictable.

4. Phase 3 — Qualitative & Causal Taxonomy
Financial facts alone do not explain why a company’s margin compressed or why its provision for
credit losses doubled. The explanations live in narrative disclosures—primarily Item 1A (Risk
Factors) and Item 7 (MD&A;)—where management is required to discuss the causal drivers of
reported results. Phase 3 of the schema introduces a qualitative causal layer that parses these
narratives into typed entity-relation pairs, producing a causal graph that complements the quantitative
fact graph.
4.1 Entity Taxonomy
The causal layer introduces seven entity types beyond the financial-fact entities established in Phases 1 and 2.
Each entity type captures a class of objects that narrative disclosures reference when explaining causation. The
schema deliberately restricts the entity set to seven types—a larger set would fragment the graph; a smaller set
would lose discriminative power.

4.3 Risk Factor Extraction (Item 1A)
Item 1A risk factor disclosures have a distinctive rhetorical structure: each risk factor is a numbered
sub-section beginning with a topic-defining header (e.g., “Our business is subject to the risks of raw material
cost inflation”) followed by 2–6 sentences of elaboration that name the causal entities and the relations among
them. The schema’s extraction pipeline applies a three-stage parser: (1) identify the risk factor header, (2)
extract the named entities (ProductFamily, GeographicMarket, MacroVariable, RegulatoryBody) via a
domain-tuned NER model, (3) classify the relation type via a small fine-tuned classifier trained on a

------------------------------------------------------------

hand-labeled subset of risk factor paragraphs.
A worked example: Pfizer’s FY22 10-K Item 1A contains a risk factor beginning “Our business may be
materially adversely affected by the expiration of patents and other intellectual property rights.” The parser
extracts: entity ProductFamily:PatentedDrugs, relation CREATES_EXPOSURE_TO PatentCliffRisk, with
the patent expiration dates parsed from the risk factor body and stored as attributes on the risk node. The
resulting edge, when joined to the financial fact graph, enables a query such as “which ProductFamily entities
in the pharma sector have patent expiration events in the next 36 months and what is the associated revenue at
risk.”
4.4 MD&A; Causal Chains (Item 7)
Item 7 MD&A; disclosures are denser and more causally explicit than risk factor disclosures. A typical
MD&A; paragraph explains a quarter-over-quarter or year-over-year change in a financial line item by
attributing it to two or three named drivers and one or two offsets. The canonical sentence pattern is “[Driver]
caused [direction] change of [magnitude], partially offset by [Offset].” The schema parser captures this pattern
and emits a directed weighted edge: (Driver, DRIVES, Outcome, weight=magnitude) and (Offset, OFFSETS,
Driver, weight=offset_magnitude).
A worked example from Apple’s FY22 10-K MD&A;: “The increase in cost of sales was primarily due to
higher component costs, partially offset by favorable foreign currency exchange.” The parser emits:
(ComponentCosts, DRIVES, CostOfSales, weight=positive), (FXRate, OFFSETS, ComponentCosts,
weight=partial). When joined to the financial fact graph, the analyst can query “which drivers were cited as the
primary cause of cost of sales growth in FY22” and recover a comparable cross-issuer table of named drivers.

5.1 Matrix Implications for Schema Design
Three implications follow directly from the matrix. First, the schema cannot assume a classified balance sheet.
The bank column of the balance-sheet-classification row is “unclassified,” which means the universal layer’s
concept of “CurrentAssets” must be marked optional and computed (where possible) via a sector-specific

------------------------------------------------------------

overlay rather than asserted as a directly reported fact. The schema’s integrity constraint for banks is therefore
TotalAssets = sum of as-filed asset line items (with no current/non-current decomposition); for non-banks, the
constraint is TotalAssets = CurrentAssets + NonCurrentAssets.
Second, the schema must accommodate fiscal year diversity. NVDA’s late-January FYE means its FY24 10-K
(filed February 2024) covers a fiscal year that overlaps with calendar-year FY2023 for the rest of the sample.
A cross-sector query such as “which issuer had the highest revenue growth in fiscal year 2023” must define
“fiscal year 2023” consistently—the schema uses the filer’s as-reported fiscal year designation, with a separate
calendar_year_overlap attribute that allows queries to be re-aligned to a common calendar period when
needed.
Third, the regulatory-metric dimension forces an overlay architecture. Bank regulatory metrics (Basel III
capital ratios, RWA, VaR) are reported only by JPM and GS; they have no non-bank equivalent. The schema
does not create empty nodes for AAPL/NVDA/PFE/XOM on these metrics. Instead, the bank overlay layer is
loaded only when querying bank-sector filings, and the universal layer remains compact. The same overlay
pattern applies to energy (production volumes, reserves), pharma (pipeline, FDA milestones), and tech
(segment gross margin, RPO) metrics. This is the central architectural decision that enables the schema to
scale to additional sectors without restructuring.

6.3 Dual-Track Design
The rules above produce a normalized StandardizedConcept pointer for every raw fact that matches a rule.
Critically, the raw fact is never overwritten or discarded. The schema’s graph model has two node types:
RawFact (carrying the XBRL tag, as-filed label, reported value, unit, period context, and filing source) and
StandardizedConcept (the canonical anchor, with its definition and sector applicability). Each RawFact is
linked to its target StandardizedConcept via a NORMALIZES_TO edge that also carries the Rule ID responsible
for the mapping. This design means that any cross-sector query can be executed at the StandardizedConcept
level, while any sector-specific deep dive can fall back to the RawFact level to inspect the as-filed label and
XBRL tag.
6.4 Sector-Conditional Transforms

------------------------------------------------------------

Several rules are sector-conditional: the same XBRL tag may map to different StandardizedConcepts
depending on the sector of the filing issuer. Rule R-07 is the clearest example:
us-gaap:NoninterestExpense from a bank filing maps to SC-05 (SG&A; proxy), while the same tag from a
non-bank filing (rare but possible for diversified issuers with financial subsidiaries) would be flagged for
manual review. The schema’s ingestion pipeline applies sector-conditional rules after determining the filer’s
sector from its SIC code (AAPL=3571, NVDA=3674, JPM=6021, GS=6211, PFE=2834, XOM=2911). Filers
with non-standard SIC codes or diversified businesses (e.g., GE, JNJ) trigger a multi-sector ingest that applies
each sector-conditional rule to the corresponding segment.
The rule set is designed for extensibility. Adding a new sector (REITs, insurance, broker-dealers) requires
defining the sector-specific overlay (concepts SC-41 onward), adding new rules (R-26 onward) to map the
sector’s XBRL tags to those concepts, and registering the SIC code range. The universal layer (SC-01 through
SC-36) and the existing rules (R-01 through R-25) remain unchanged. This additive extensibility is the central
design property that makes the schema “Universal” rather than sector-specific.
CHAPTER SEVEN · DELIVERABLE
7. Edge Case Register (Output 3)
The third formal deliverable is the Edge Case Register. SEC filings are not static; they are revised,
restated, reclassified, and recast. The schema must accommodate these mutations without losing
provenance, without overwriting history, and without breaking cross-period queries. This chapter
covers the four core edge cases the schema is designed to handle: restatements, quarterly revisions,
non-calendar fiscal years, and discontinued operations.
7.1 Restatements (Form 10-K/A, 10-Q/A)
A restatement is the correction of a previously issued financial statement due to an error identified under ASC
250 (Accounting Changes and Error Corrections). Restatements are filed as amendments (Form 10-K/A or
10-Q/A) and are flagged in the cover page of the amendment with the reason for the restatement. The schema
models restatements by preserving the original fact with its original as-filed value and creating a new
revised_fact node linked to the original via a RESTATES edge. The revised fact carries the corrected value,
the restatement reason (parsed from the amendment’s explanatory note), and the restatement effective date.
ASC 250 distinguishes between correction of errors (restatements) and changes in accounting principle
(retrospective adjustments). The schema represents these differently: an error correction produces a RESTATES
edge (high severity, audit trail required); a change in accounting principle produces a
RETROSPECTIVELY_RECASTS edge (lower severity, expected and disclosed). A third type, the revision
(immaterial correction), produces a REVISION_OF edge with a materiality flag set to “immaterial.” The query
layer can then filter by severity: a compliance officer queries for all RESTATES edges in the past 24 months; an
analyst may choose to include REVISION_OF edges with materiality = immaterial.
A worked example: in 2020, several large banks restated their credit loss allowances following the transition to
CECL (ASU 2016-13). JPMorgan’s restatement is modeled as a RETROSPECTIVELY_RECASTS edge from the
post-CECL allowance fact to the pre-CECL allowance fact, with the restatement reason attribute set to “CECL

------------------------------------------------------------

adoption.” This preserves both the pre-CECL and post-CECL values, enabling time-series queries that span the
transition. A naive schema that overwrote the original fact with the restated value would lose the historical
transition information that is essential for understanding the CECL impact.
7.2 Quarterly Revisions
Form 10-Q amendments are more frequent than 10-K amendments and span a wider range of materiality. The
SEC’s threshold for what constitutes a material restatement (requiring an amended filing) versus an immaterial
revision (disclosed in the subsequent periodic filing) is not codified as a single numeric threshold; it depends
on the qualitative and quantitative impact assessed by management and the auditor. The schema captures this
distinction via the materiality_flag attribute on the revision edge, with values “material,” “immaterial,” or
“undetermined.”
A common revision pattern: a 10-Q filed in Q1 reports preliminary numbers that are refined in the Q2 10-Q’s
comparative-period column. The schema models this as a REVISION_OF edge from the Q2 fact to the Q1 fact,
with materiality determined by the magnitude of the change. Materiality is computed against two reference
points: the absolute magnitude of the change as a percentage of the original value, and the magnitude as a
percentage of net income. Either crossing a 5% threshold triggers a material flag; both below 2% triggers
immaterial; the in-between range triggers undetermined and requires manual review.
7.3 Non-Calendar Fiscal Years
Two of the six issuers in the sample operate on non-calendar fiscal years: Apple’s fiscal year ends on the last
Saturday of September (so FY22 ends October 1, 2022, and FY23 ends September 30, 2023), and NVIDIA’s
fiscal year ends on the last Sunday of January (so FY24 ends January 28, 2024, and FY25 ends January 26,
2025). The remaining four issuers (JPM, GS, PFE, XOM) all use December 31 fiscal year-ends. The schema
models fiscal periods as explicit nodes with three attributes: fiscal_year (the filer’s as-reported designation,
e.g., “FY24”), fiscal_period_end_date (the actual calendar date the period ended), and
calendar_year_overlap (the calendar year that contains the majority of the fiscal period’s days).
A cross-issuer query for “revenue growth in calendar year 2023” requires alignment across the fiscal periods.
The schema’s query layer provides a align_to_calendar_year() function that, for each issuer, selects the
fiscal period whose calendar_year_overlap equals 2023. For AAPL, that is FY23 (ending September 30,
2023); for NVDA, that is FY23 (ending January 29, 2023) and FY24 (ending January 28, 2024) — the schema
picks FY24 because more of its 365 days fall in calendar 2023 than in calendar 2024. For JPM, GS, PFE,
XOM, the choice is trivial: FY ending December 31, 2023.
A second implication of non-calendar fiscal years is the reporting lag. Apple typically files its 10-K within 30
days of fiscal year-end (early November for a late-September FYE); NVIDIA files within 30 days of its
late-January FYE (late February). The banks file within 40–50 days of December 31. The schema captures the
reporting lag as a reporting_lag_in_days attribute on each Filing node, enabling queries that adjust for the
information asymmetry across issuers—a fact reported by NVDA on February 22, 2024 (FY24 10-K) is more
contemporaneously informative than a fact reported by JPM on February 22, 2024 (10-K for the year ended
December 31, 2023), because the NVDA fact reflects a fiscal period that ended January 28, 2024.

------------------------------------------------------------

7.4 Discontinued Operations (ASC 205-20)
ASC 205-20 requires that discontinued operations be reported separately from continuing operations on the
income statement, with separate disclosure of the assets and liabilities held for sale or disposed of. The schema
models discontinued operations via a separate DiscontinuedOpsSegment entity linked to the issuer’s Company
node. Income statement facts associated with the discontinued operation are tagged with a
continuing_ops_flag = false, distinguishing them from continuing operations facts.
The harder schema problem is retrospective recasting. When an operation is classified as discontinued in the
current period, ASC 205-20 requires that all prior-period comparatives be recast to exclude the discontinued
operation. The schema handles this by preserving both the as-originally-reported and as-recast versions of each
prior-period fact: the as-originally-reported fact has presentation_basis=as_originally_reported; the
as-recast fact has presentation_basis=as_recast_excluding_discontinued. Queries that compare
across periods must explicitly select which presentation basis to use; the schema’s query layer defaults to the
as-recast basis for continuity but preserves the as-originally-reported values for audit trail purposes.
A worked example: when Pfizer completed the spin-off of its Upjohn business (combined with Mylan to form
Viatris) in November 2020, the FY21 10-K presented prior-period comparatives with Upjohn excluded. The
schema preserves both: the FY19 revenue as-originally-reported includes Upjohn; the FY19 revenue as-recast
excludes Upjohn. A query for “Pfizer FY19 total revenue” returns two values unless the query explicitly
specifies presentation basis. The schema’s default is to return the most-recently-recast version, which is what
an analyst comparing FY19 to FY21 would typically want.

8.3 Implementation Notes
The schema is graph-engine-agnostic. In a property-graph implementation (Neo4j, Memgraph), each node
type becomes a label, each relation type becomes a relationship type, and properties are stored inline. In an
RDF implementation (Stardog, GraphDB), node types become classes (with subclasses for sector overlays),
relation types become OWL object properties, and cardinality constraints are encoded via owl:minCardinality
and owl:maxCardinality axioms. The schema’s design avoids features that would tie it to a specific engine:
there are no computed properties (computed at query time), no engine-specific path expressions (queries use
standard graph traversal patterns), and no engine-specific indexing hints (left to the implementation layer).
A note on COMPOUNDS: this relation is technically a hyperedge (three participants rather than two), which is not
natively supported by property-graph engines. The schema recommends modeling this as a CompoundEffect
node with three incoming relations (COMPOUND_FACTOR from each contributing factor) and one outgoing
relation (COMPOUNDS_OUTCOME to the outcome). RDF engines can model this natively as a reified statement.
CHAPTER NINE
9. Validation & Ingestion Recommendations
A schema is only as useful as the validation that enforces it. This chapter describes how a graph
architect should operationalize the Universal Financial Graph Schema: the ingestion pipeline, the
validation rules, the provenance tracking, the versioning strategy, and the testing approach.
9.1 Ingestion Pipeline
The recommended ingestion pipeline has five stages. Stage 1 is raw acquisition: pull filings from EDGAR via
the EDGAR FULL-TEXT Search API or direct URL construction, parse the XBRL instance document, and
extract every fact with its context, unit, and as-filed label. Stage 2 is raw fact node creation: for each extracted
fact, create a RawFact node with all attributes populated, linked to the Filing node and the Section node it was
reported in. Stage 3 is normalization: apply each of the 25 rules (R-01 through R-25) to each RawFact; for
each match, create a NORMALIZES_TO edge from the RawFact to the target StandardizedConcept node,
carrying the rule_id as an edge property. Stage 4 is causal extraction: parse Item 1A and Item 7 narrative text,
extract entity mentions and typed causal relations, create the corresponding entity nodes (ProductFamily,
MacroVariable, etc.) and CausalRelation edges. Stage 5 is validation: run the integrity constraints described in
Section 9.2 and flag any violations for review.
9.2 Validation Rules
The schema enforces three classes of validation rules. Universal constraints apply to all sectors and all filers:
TotalAssets = TotalLiabilities + TotalEquity (within $1 tolerance for rounding); NetIncome ≤
IncomeBeforeTax (signed comparison); CashFromOperations + CashFromInvesting +
CashFromFinancing = NetChangeInCash. Sector-specific constraints apply only to filings of that sector: for
banks, LoansHeldForInvestmentNet + AllowanceForCreditLosses =
GrossLoansHeldForInvestment; for non-banks, CurrentAssets + NonCurrentAssets = TotalAssets
(where classified balance sheet is reported). Temporal constraints apply across filing periods:

------------------------------------------------------------

fiscal_quarter Q4 → fiscal_year end consistency; comparative_period_in_N_filing ==
reported_period_in_N-1_filing (with tolerance for restatements).
9.3 Provenance Tracking
Every normalized fact retains a complete provenance chain: the RawFact node carries the XBRL tag, the
as-filed label, the reported value, the unit, the period context, and a pointer to the Filing node (which carries
the accession number, filing date, and form type). The NORMALIZES_TO edge carries the Rule ID
responsible for the mapping. This means that any analytical result can be traced back to its source: a query for
“Apple FY22 TotalRevenue” returns the StandardizedConcept value, which links to the RawFact, which links
to the Filing, which links to the EDGAR accession number, which links to the as-filed document. An auditor
or compliance officer can drill from any analytical result to the underlying primary source in a single graph
traversal.
9.4 Versioning Strategy
When a restatement arrives, the schema does not overwrite the original fact. Instead, it creates a revised
RawFact node and links it to the original via a RESTATES edge carrying the RestatementEvent properties
(restatement_type, materiality, restatement_reason, effective_date). Queries that ask for “the value of
TotalRevenue for Apple FY22” must specify which version they want: the as-originally-reported version, the
as-restated version, or the most-recently-reported version (the default). This versioning strategy preserves the
full audit trail while keeping the default query experience simple—most analysts want the
most-recently-reported value, which is what the query layer returns unless told otherwise.
9.5 Testing Strategy
The schema should be tested against a curated set of filings with known answers: the “golden set.” The golden
set should include at least two filings per sector (one clean filing, one with restatements or unusual
disclosures), spanning the 2018–2026 window. For each golden filing, the expected StandardizedConcept
values, the expected causal extraction edges, and the expected integrity constraint checks should be
pre-computed and stored. Ingestion of each golden filing should reproduce the expected outputs within
tolerance; any deviation triggers a test failure that must be resolved before the schema is deployed to
production.
A specific test case worth highlighting: the CECL transition at JPMorgan (FY20 10-K). The pre-CECL
allowance for credit losses (reported under the incurred-loss model) and the post-CECL allowance (reported
under the expected-loss model) must both be preserved with explicit transition annotations. The test verifies
that a query for “JPM allowance for credit losses FY19” returns both values (as-originally-reported and
as-restated for CECL comparability) and that the RESTATES edge between them carries restatement_reason
= “CECL adoption.” This single test exercises the schema’s restatement handling, sector overlay loading, and
provenance tracking simultaneously.

CHAPTER TEN
10. Limitations & Future Work
This document is explicit about its scope and the boundaries of the schema it presents. A graph
architect evaluating the schema for production use should weigh these limitations against the
schema’s coverage before committing.
10.1 Scope Limitations
The research corpus is illustrative rather than exhaustive. Six issuers across four sectors cannot surface every
structural pattern that exists in the EDGAR universe. Specifically absent from the sample: insurance
companies (which carry their own statutory accounting overlays), broker-dealers (regulated by FINRA and the
SEC’s Rule 17a-5), REITs (which require segment-level NOI reconciliation), foreign private issuers (which
file Form 20-F under IFRS or home-country GAAP), and private companies (which file Reg D Form D
notices but no periodic reports). The schema’s overlay architecture is designed to extend cleanly to these
sectors—each requires a new overlay table (§9.6 of the brief), new StandardizedConcepts (SC-41 onward),
and new normalization rules (R-26 onward)—but the work of defining those overlays is out of scope for this
document.
The 2018–2026 window captures the most turbulent period in modern SEC disclosure history, but it does not
capture pre-CECL banking practice (pre-2020), pre-COVID supply chain assumptions (pre-2020), or
pre-IFRS-16 lease accounting (pre-2019). A schema designed to ingest historical filings back to, say, 2010
would need to add overlays for these accounting regimes. The good news: the schema’s dual-track design (raw
fact preserved alongside normalized concept) means that historical filings can be ingested even when their
XBRL tags fall outside the current rule set—they will simply lack normalized edges, which the query layer can
flag with a normalization_status = unmapped attribute.
10.2 Language and GAAP Scope
The schema is English-only and US-GAAP-only. Foreign private issuers file Form 20-F with financial
statements prepared under IFRS (most common), home-country GAAP (e.g., Japanese GAAP), or a
reconciliation to US GAAP. The XBRL tags under IFRS are drawn from the IFRS taxonomy, not the
US-GAAP taxonomy, so the 25 normalization rules in Chapter 6 do not apply. A future IFRS overlay would
define a parallel set of StandardizedConcepts (or, preferably, map IFRS-specific XBRL tags to the same
canonical SC-01 through SC-40 list, preserving the universal layer’s cross-GAAP queryability).
10.3 Causal Extraction Maturity
The Phase 3 causal extraction pipeline (Chapter 4) is presented as a schema design, not as a production-ready
implementation. The three-stage parser—header detection, named entity recognition, relation
classification—is conceptually sound but requires a hand-labeled training corpus of several thousand risk
factor and MD&A; paragraphs to achieve production-grade accuracy. The schema deliberately does not
prescribe a specific NLP architecture; the choice of model (transformer fine-tune, LLM-prompted extraction,

------------------------------------------------------------

rule-based hybrid) is an implementation decision. What the schema prescribes is the output: typed edges with
explicit source quotes, weights, and magnitude estimates, all carried as edge properties for downstream
validation.
10.4 Future Work
Three extensions are highest priority. First, the IFRS reconciliation layer: define a mapping table from IFRS
taxonomy tags to the existing StandardizedConcepts, enabling cross-GAAP queries across US-domestic and
foreign private issuers. Second, automated 8-K event-to-periodic-filing linkage: today the schema relies on a
SUPERSEDES / CONFIRMS / REFINES edge set that must be manually created or inferred via simple
date-proximity heuristics; a learned classifier that links 8-K events to their subsequent 10-Q / 10-K
confirmations would close this gap. Third, temporal causal inference: extending the causal layer to track how
DRIVES and IMPACTS_MARGIN edges evolve across filings—a risk factor cited once in 2020 and then dropped
in 2021 carries a different signal than one cited persistently across five filings.
Longer-term, the schema should be extended to cover the SEC’s proposed climate disclosure rules (currently in
rulemaking limbo but likely to be revisited), which would introduce a new class of disclosure (Scope 1, 2, 3
emissions; climate risk assessment) requiring new StandardizedConcepts and new sector overlays. The same
applies to the SEC’s proposed human capital disclosure rules, which would require a HumanCapital overlay
with sector-specific metrics (banking: headcount by branch; technology: R&D; headcount as % of total;
energy: workforce safety metrics).
